How Cameron Thinks (In His Own Frame)

  • "Fixed dollars in, defensible cash flow out." The core venture archetype he underwrites. Best example: nuclear firms don't operate reactors — they spend fixed capital to license designs, then collect fees for the ~40-year reactor lifetime. High-margin, cash-flowing, defensible.
  • Hardware that becomes software. GenLogs sells sensors upfront, but once the network is built it "looks like a software business" — a real-time tracking layer over the majority of US freight. He likes hardware with a network/data flywheel.
  • Industrial readiness > product-market fit. In infra, "wanting" the product is irrelevant if it fails grid compatibility, procurement specs, or lifetime performance. He funds readiness models: a credible, milestone-based 12–24 month path to a bankable milestone (pilot runtime, offtake, facility financing). Reverse-engineers from the buyer's spec.
  • Deep tech = tech risk, not market risk. "If it's taking both tech and market risk, it's probably a problem." Deep tech should carry limited market risk; the underwriting is about credible technical milestones.
  • Demand-side > supply-side policy. Guaranteed demand (Saudi "we'll buy X at price Y," or DOD/NASA committing to chip volumes) beats IRA-style supply subsidies. Revenue guarantees unlock private financing for first-of-a-kind facilities — the "valley of death" bridge.
  • AI = rate of exploration. Rejects the Thiel "AI favors centralized regimes" framing. His counter: AI lets one person do what used to need a team (CAD, code, mechanical design) → more shots on goal. Restricting model access constrains American innovation. ("Be the Mighty Ducks, not the centralized Icelandic team.")
